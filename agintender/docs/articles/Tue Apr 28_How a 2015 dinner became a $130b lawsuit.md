How Sam Altman ran the longest psyop in tech history, and why Elon walked into it with both eyes open
February 2015. Mission District, San Francisco. Y Combinator HQ, third floor.
It's that drizzling California winter rain — not heavy, just enough to smear every neon sign on the block into a blur. In the corner office, Sam Altman pushes his fourth cold americano aside. The cursor blinks under one sentence for a long time:
"AI is the biggest existential threat to humanity."
Delete. Type. Delete. Type again.
He isn't picking words. He's picking a side. Specifically: whether to use a sentence he doesn't fully believe to leverage a guy he very much needs.
Three months earlier. Rosewood Sand Hill, Menlo Park. The default location for SV power dinners — lighting just dim enough to make everyone feel like they're inside some secret history. Eight people at the table. One center of gravity:
Elon Musk.
Black tee, chair tilted back ~30 degrees — that fake-casual posture that's actually him looking down at the table. Steak untouched. He's been on the same topic since appetizers:
DeepMind.
"You know what happened after Google bought DeepMind?" His voice isn't loud but it carries — the kind of carry you build standing on SpaceX launch pads yelling into wind. "Larry told me how he thinks about AI. He thinks AI should just be free to develop. He literally said — if AI is smarter than humans, maybe AI should be the one making decisions. He was laughing when he said it. His eyes weren't laughing."
People nod politely. Cut their steaks. Only Altman is sitting completely still, one hand on the table edge, wineglass untouched, locked onto Musk's mouth with this almost invisible focus.
Not listening to the content. Listening to the frequency.
Musk keeps going: "I invested in DeepMind, you know that? Not for returns. So I could see what they were doing. I needed someone on the inside. Now Google owns it and I can't even get in the door."
Finally picks up his fork. Cuts a piece. Holds it midair without eating it.
"If nobody does something — and I mean now, not in five years, not in ten — if nobody builds a counterweight outside Google, our generation is going to make the biggest mistake in human history."
Altman puts down the wine.
He's running a read. The guy across from him has more money than almost anyone alive, runs rockets, EVs, solar — and his pupils are showing something none of those assets can fix:
Fear.
Not theatrical fear. Not pitch-deck fear. The kind that pulls you out of bed at 3am and makes you stare at the ceiling.
Prologue: the price of a blog post
Back at his Nob Hill apartment, Altman writes one line in his notes app. Never makes a public record. But the logic runs through everything that comes next:
His fear is real. Which means he can be guided.
February 26, 2015. The blog post drops. "AI is the biggest existential threat to humanity" flies across the internet like a guided missile aimed at exactly one target. (https://blog.samaltman.com/machine-intelligence-part-1)
 
Within four hours, Musk's text comes in. Altman is in the office talking YC batch picks with a partner. Glances at the phone. Doesn't reply. Lets the notification sit there for 17 minutes — not because he's busy, but because when you're fishing, you don't yank the line the second the fish bites.
Text says: "Let's talk."
December 2015, last Friday of the month. OpenAI is announced from a temporary SF office. The press release went through seven drafts. Final version: non-profit AI research company, mission to ensure AGI benefits all of humanity.
Musk commits tens of millions. Altman stands next to him as he signs the check. People in the room remember Altman's expression as warm, almost grateful.
Nobody catches that the smile briefly disappears in the half-second Musk looks down to sign.
Two takes on this origin point:
Karen Hao's read (300+ interviews, ~90 current/former OpenAI staff): Altman was mirroring Musk's fear from day one. He didn't necessarily believe AI was the biggest threat — he just precisely cloned Musk's vocabulary to extract trust and capital. Calculated manipulation.
OpenAI's frame (Jan 2026 statement): doesn't directly address it, but implies both founders genuinely shared the AI-risk concern in 2015. The split came later — not over belief, over control.
Act I: the honeymoon
Mission District. Pioneer Building, top floor.
The previous tenant was a craft brewery. There are still beer stains on the floor. The kitchen smells faintly of malt sometimes. This is OpenAI at peak idealism.
Researchers are leaving Google Brain, FAIR, DeepMind to take 40-60% pay cuts. Some new hires literally sleep on the office couch because they relocated faster than they could find apartments. Whiteboards in hallways with hand-written equations. Fridge contents: La Croix, leftover pizza, one carton of blueberries someone abandoned three weeks ago.
Ilya Sutskever — chief scientist, Hinton's protégé — shows up at 9am sharp every day. But not to his desk. He goes to the whiteboard first and stands there for 20 minutes. He has a ritual: before thinking about a new problem, he erases everything from yesterday. Slowly. Like a ceremony. Then stands in front of the empty board, arms crossed, head slightly tilted, lips moving silently.
A new intern once asked him: "What are you thinking about when you stand there?"
Ilya looked at him completely seriously: "I'm thinking about whether the human brain is a statistical machine. If it is, we can build one. If it isn't —" he paused, "if it isn't, that's even more interesting."
Greg Brockman, CTO. Different vibe. Pure pragmatist engineer. Three laptops always open on his desk running different jobs. Quiet. When he does talk, he pushes his glasses up his nose first — a tic that becomes a stress-meter in later board meetings.
Musk shows up every 2-3 weeks. Sits in the parking lot for 10 minutes before coming in. Not on a call. Not reading. Just sitting. People who know him recognize this: he's switching modes. Tesla CEO mode → SpaceX CEO mode → OpenAI board mode. Five companies' OS running in parallel. Buffer time required between context switches.
First thing he does inside: find Ilya. They stand at the whiteboard forever. Musk asks. Ilya draws. Once during a transformer attention discussion, Musk cut in: "When you say 'attention,' do you mean the same thing as human attention?"
Ilya thought for a long time. "Not exactly. But maybe… the gap is smaller than we think."
Musk went silent. Later wrote in an internal email: "If Ilya's right — if intelligence is just a scaling problem — then what we're doing is more dangerous than I thought."
Altman in these scenes is basically invisible. Not at the whiteboard. Not on papers. Not writing code. He's doing the other job: scheduling meetings, lease negotiations, legal docs, candidate interviews, lunches with reporters. He's the backend service making sure the frontend looks smooth.
But if you watch carefully — like Hao did, reconstructing this period through 300 interviews — Altman's also doing something else.
He's mapping everyone's weak spot.
Ilya's weak spot: research freedom. As long as nobody touches his direction, he doesn't care who's CEO.
Greg's weak spot: order. He needs predictable structure to function. Chaos doubles his glasses-pushing rate.
Musk's weak spot — Altman knew this from Rosewood Sand Hill — is fear. Real, deep fear of AI eating humanity.
A man's beliefs are armor. A man's fear is a parts list.
And Altman is patiently labeling each part.
But Musk isn't a passive sponsor either. Even during the honeymoon, he never treats OpenAI as a real independent non-profit. Early 2017, he does something Hao specifically flags: he asks OpenAI to send a team to fix Tesla's Autopilot.
This request is incoherent in a non-profit context. OpenAI's mission is safe AI for humanity, not technical support for Tesla's commercial product. But nobody says no to Musk. Scott Gray, Ilya himself, Greg, eventually Andrej Karpathy — all spend serious time on Autopilot.
Karpathy ends up getting fully poached. Goes to Tesla as AI director.
A core researcher at a non-profit, recruited by its biggest donor for a commercial project. Nobody talks about this publicly at the time. But it reveals a structural fact that was true from day one: Musk never took OpenAI's "independence" seriously. In his head, OpenAI was a piece on his board — an important piece, but a piece.
Two reads:
Karen Hao: Altman's mapping power under cover of admin work. Musk asking OpenAI to fix Autopilot + poaching Karpathy = he was already extracting commercial value from a non-profit.
OpenAI (in the Jan 2026 statement, directly): "Elon never really treated OpenAI as an independent non-profit." He "was already using OpenAI for his own commercial interests before any for-profit conversations." This is one of the few paragraphs where OpenAI directly attacks Musk's early behavior, and the message is: you weren't doing charity from day one — what right do you have to call us traitors now?
Act II: the cracks
October 2017. SF gets a full week of overcast.
OpenAI's GPU spend has 4x'd in six months. Ilya's launching an ambitious new project that needs more compute than the entire org has ever used. He walks into Altman's office with that specific frown — corners of the mouth slightly down, like a mathematician forced to admit he can't solve an equation. Altman has seen this face more than once.
"Sam, I need more compute."
"How much?"
Ilya names a number. Altman doesn't reply right away. Swivels the chair 45 degrees. Stares at the window. Pioneer Building looks out on Mission's painted Victorians, the clouds beyond.
"Ilya, you know what our bank account looks like right now?"
"I know. But if we don't do this, Google will."
That's Ilya's killshot. He rarely talks competition. But every time he says "Google," there's an unusual urgency. In Ilya's head this isn't a corporate war — it's a religious race over who creates the digital deity first. If Google gets there first with weak safety, game over.
Altman swivels back. "I'll figure something out."
But where? OpenAI is a non-profit. Can't raise. Can't IPO. Can't sell equity. All money comes from donations. The number of people willing to donate tens of millions to an AI lab fits on two hands.
What's keeping him up at night is this: the biggest of those donors is becoming impossible to manage.
Musk's mood that period is visibly degrading. Tesla Model 3 is in "production hell" — Fremont line creating new disasters daily. Musk sleeps on the factory floor in a sleeping bag. SpaceX prepping Falcon Heavy. Neuralink. Boring Company. All eating his bandwidth.
Every OpenAI board meeting he shows up to, he looks more cooked than the last. His remarks get shorter, sharper, more impatient. He starts directly questioning technical decisions in meetings — "why this architecture?", "what's the cost-benefit?" — questions that work fine at SpaceX engineering reviews but in a basic research lab sound like a non-expert interrogating experts.
Ilya starts closing his office door on Musk-visit days. Greg's glasses-push frequency hits new highs.
Late 2017 — exact date varies across sources, Hao places it "somewhere late fall to early winter" — Musk asks Altman to meet 1:1 in Palo Alto.
It's at one of Musk's PA properties. Not the biggest one (already sold). A relatively low-key colonial. White exterior. Two perfectly symmetrical oak trees out front. Musk's houses have one feature in common: minimal decor, almost no furniture, walls bare. Visitors say it feels like sitting inside an empty space capsule.
Thursday night. Altman drives 40 minutes from SF. Parks his Tesla Model S out front. Sits in the car for ~5 minutes before getting out. He later told a close friend about those five minutes: "I knew what he was going to say. I was working out how to react."
Musk waiting in the living room. Two glasses of water on the coffee table. No snacks. Half the lights on. Musk on the far left of the couch, leaning forward, hands clasped on knees — his serious-conversation posture, opposite of the leaned-back-don't-care posture he uses in public.
"Sam, at our current burn, we don't survive two years."
"I know."
"And Google — Sundar just signed off another few billion for DeepMind. What are we fighting them with? Jeff and Reid combined don't cover six months of GPU bills."
(Jeff = Bezos, early small donor, never deeply involved. Reid = Hoffman, key early backer.) The fact that Musk can rattle off names + corresponding amounts means he was watching OpenAI's books closer than anyone realized.
"I have a plan," Musk says.
He stands up. At SpaceX, when Musk stands up from a chair, every engineer in the room knows what's coming next isn't a suggestion — it's an order.
"Hand OpenAI to me. I'll run it as CEO. Or — we fold it into Tesla. Tesla has cash. Tesla has GPU clusters. Tesla has the best AI engineering team in the world from Autopilot. The OpenAI research team plugs straight into Tesla infrastructure. Compute problem solved overnight."
He walks to the window. Back to Altman. PA street outside is movie-set quiet.
"This isn't about what I want, Sam. It's math. We don't have enough money. Google has infinite money. Without a structural change, in 18 months OpenAI is a second-tier lab and then it dies. I'm not watching that happen."
Altman is on the other end of the couch. ~1.5 meters apart physically. Psychologically, the distance is its own weather system.
He doesn't speak immediately. Looks like he's just listening. Actually he's processing three layers in parallel:
Content layer: Musk wants control. Motive layer: Musk genuinely believes only he can save humanity from AI. Not arrogance — the logical endpoint of fear. When you actually believe AI will end humanity, your instinct isn't "let's discuss." It's "I have to be in charge." Strategy layer: The actual game. If Musk gets control, Sam Altman becomes an employee. Not a regular employee. An employee under Musk — a guy famous for firing people like changing socks.
"Elon, this is a big idea. I need time to think. Need to talk to Ilya and Greg."
"Don't drag it out."
Altman drives back to SF. 101 lights streak across the windshield. No music. No calls. He's working out the next move.
He doesn't need time to "think." He needs time to operate.

Act III: the call — two versions
Some night in September 2017. A three-way call.
On the line: Musk, Ilya, Greg. The contents of this call entered legal discovery via Musk's lawsuit. Full transcript is public. It became the most disputed piece of evidence in the whole fight — because Musk and OpenAI read completely opposite stories out of the same recording.
Actual call content:
Greg: "How much of your time should we plan for over the next few weeks?"
Musk: "Top priority. We need to figure out how to transition from non-profit to — something that's still essentially a charity but can be a B-corp or C-corp or something. We need to tell the story right. Can't lose the moral high ground. This is absolutely critical."
Ilya: "I have some ideas. One option: keep the non-profit, set up a C-corp alongside. But I don't love it — C-corp is unconstrained in some ways. Other option: define the mission as minimizing existential risk by building friendly AGI. I prefer this — a brand new entity, fundamentally mission-aligned."
Musk: "Agreed, that's better. I'd never even heard of B-corp before — Sam brought it up. But it sounds right. Non-profit shouldn't shut down. Should continue in some form."
Ilya: "Any preference on the name?"
Musk: "OpenAI. Mission continuity. Same thing."
Ilya: "Fine, as long as the entity has some fundamentally charitable nature."
Musk: "Alright, I'm really excited to do this with you guys."
Ilya: "Just one small thing left — actually doing it."
Musk: "Okay, cool. I'll figure out the full B-corp details tomorrow, kick off the process. We'll stay in close touch and get this done fast."
End of call.
This call is the Rashomon of the whole dispute.
Musk's courtroom narrative: His lawyers cherry-picked two phrases — "essentially a charity" and "continue in some form" — to argue Musk always insisted OpenAI stay non-profit. Court filing says Musk insisted any new entity must "support the non-profit's mission" and OpenAI should remain "essentially a charity."
OpenAI's counter (Jan 2026 public statement): they posted the full transcript and red-marked everything Musk's lawyers omitted. Argument: Musk wasn't just not opposing the for-profit conversion, he was the most aggressive driver of it. Literal quote: "transition from non-profit to B-corp or C-corp," and "really excited" to "kick off the process tomorrow." More importantly, the person who said the new entity should be "fundamentally charitable" was Ilya — not Musk. Musk only said the non-profit should "continue in some form" — which is almost exactly what OpenAI's current architecture is (a public benefit corp + a preserved non-profit foundation). OpenAI also points out that Musk himself personally created an OpenAI public benefit corp entity shortly after this call.
Karen Hao's read: she doesn't get into the wording fight. Her argument is one level up: regardless of what Musk said in 2017, his core demand was always control. For-profit, non-profit — both means to an end. The end was making sure he sat in the driver's seat. Hao surfaces a detail that's not in the transcript but emerged in later negotiations: Musk demanded majority equity, full control, even talked about his kids inheriting control of AGI.
From OpenAI's POV, Musk wasn't dragged into for-profit conversion. He was dragging them into it.
Not passive. Not reluctant. Active, hot, and impatient.
When Greg opens the call asking the scheduling question, Musk's answer is "top priority" — that word from his mouth carries the same urgency as a SpaceX launch countdown. He's not discussing an option. He's issuing an order.
When Ilya lays out his thoughts, Musk jumps in with "agreed, that's better. I hadn't even heard of B-corp before — Sam mentioned it. Sounds right. Non-profit shouldn't shut down. Should continue in some form."
"Continue in some form" — remember this phrase. Years later in court Musk will claim he always insisted OpenAI must stay non-profit. The actual recording says "in some form" — vague enough that it lines up almost exactly with OpenAI's current structure (public benefit corp + preserved non-profit foundation).
End of call, Musk says something that doesn't get cited in legal filings but matters for the historical narrative:
"Alright, I'm really excited to do this with you guys."
Ilya hits back with that dry humor of his: "Just one small thing left — actually doing it."
Musk laughs: "Okay, cool. I'll figure out the full B-corp details tomorrow, kick off the process. Let's stay in close touch and get this done fast."
If the story stopped here, it'd be a warm one about three idealists trying to find a sustainable future. But it didn't stop. Because the next phase of negotiation revealed what Musk's "excitement" was actually about.
Act IV: $80 billion and Mars
Late September to October 2017. Negotiations go deep.
What started as an architecture conversation quickly turns into a power conversation. When the topic shifts to who owns how much equity, who has final decision rights — Musk shows his hand:
He demands majority equity. He demands full control.
His justifications come in layers. Layer 1 — commercial logic: "I put in the most money, took the most risk, I should have proportional control." Layer 2 — historical lesson: "I got burned not having control before" — referring to getting kicked out of CEO at PayPal — "not making that mistake again."
But Layer 3 is what cooled the room.
When the conversation hits succession — what happens if Musk one day stops running the entity, who takes over — Musk's answer drops the temperature several degrees:
His kids.
He wants his kids to inherit control of AGI.
This isn't a corporate governance answer. This is a dynastic answer.
And — per details OpenAI later disclosed — Musk in this same period revealed his bigger plan: he needs to accumulate $80 billion in personal wealth because he's going to build a self-sustaining city on Mars. AGI is a key piece of that plan. In his macro narrative, controlling AGI isn't the goal — it's the vehicle. The vehicle to Mars. (https://openai.com/index/the-truth-elon-left-out/)
Which is why one of Musk's former colleagues, when consulted by the OpenAI team, flagged a pattern worth noting: Musk's Mars plan also originally appeared as a charity — a concept called "Mars Oasis," meant to inspire public excitement about space. Later it became SpaceX, a tens-of-billions commercial empire.
That same former colleague flagged another pattern: Musk has a tendency to "demonize" people who leave his companies.
Altman didn't need anyone to tell him this. He'd been assembling this picture since the Rosewood Sand Hill dinner. All the puzzle pieces are now in place: Musk doesn't want to protect AGI. He wants to own AGI — same way he owns SpaceX, Tesla, X. The "save humanity" framing is the moral wrapper around the control grab.
Two reads:
Karen Hao: Per Hao's reconstruction from multiple insider interviews, Musk gave layered justifications. Layer 1 = commercial. Layer 2 = PayPal trauma. Layer 3 = succession-via-children, which she frames as the dynastic tell. Hao also surfaces the $80B-for-Mars detail and the Mars Oasis → SpaceX charity-to-commercial pattern. Plus the colleague who warned Musk demonizes departing employees.
OpenAI's evidence: confirmed the same details in the Jan 2026 statement, in cooler legal language: "Although his court filings obscured these details, the negotiations were in fact very intense, involving deep private conversations. Elon said he needed $80 billion to build a self-sustaining city on Mars, said he needed and deserved majority equity. Said he needed full control because not having it had burned him before. On succession, he surprisingly raised the idea of his children controlling AGI." OpenAI also disclosed a key detail: Musk asked the OpenAI team to consult his "references" — people who'd worked closely with him — for background. One of those references flagged the Mars project's charity-to-commercial transition pattern, and that Musk demonizes ex-employees.
Negotiations stuck here. Greg and Ilya weren't handing full control to Musk. Altman especially wasn't.
So Musk changed strategies.
Act V: three pieces on the board
Early October 2017.
Musk realizes direct negotiation is jammed — Greg and Ilya aren't agreeing to majority equity + full control. So he pivots.
He uses his OpenAI liaison Shivon Zilis (young VC, famously high-EQ) to relay a new offer to Greg and Ilya: if you drop the B-corp idea and commit to staying non-profit, I'll keep funding. But in exchange — give me two more board seats, sign a no-quit pledge for X period, and a non-compete.
Translation: surrender your ability to walk, in exchange I keep cutting checks.
Greg and Ilya don't reject it immediately. They actually consider it seriously — not because it's appealing, but because they morally feel they shouldn't quit on Musk without trying every option. Ilya says something at one point that makes Altman's eyebrow twitch: "I think morally we shouldn't push Elon out. We should try to make non-profit work, try to convince him to stay."
But they have two unresolvable concerns: (1) can non-profit actually raise enough? (2) if they sign the lockup and Musk turns around and starts his own AGI project inside Tesla — which he's already hinted at — they're trapped in an underfunded non-profit watching Musk build the thing they were supposed to build, somewhere else.
Through Shivon they ask: any chance of doing B-corp together with Elon? Shivon comes back: Musk says yes but needs 6-9 months of cooldown — let the negotiation heat dissipate, plus he's focused on Model 3 ramp.
Few weeks later, Musk changes his mind. Starts saying the for-profit option failing is actually "a lucky accident" because it could never raise enough anyway. Pivots to: folding OpenAI into Tesla is the only way to fulfill the mission. Hints that regardless of what OpenAI does, he'll push AGI inside Tesla.
By early November, Greg and Ilya are looking for an exit that doesn't end at Tesla.
Greg starts journaling. Not literary, introspective journaling. Engineer-style: fast, bullet-pointed, like a debug log of every day's status and decisions.
These journals later entered legal discovery via Musk's lawsuit. They're some of the most vivid first-person material in the entire story.
November 6, 2017 is the key entry.
It opens with fragmented discussion notes. Sam shows up in the record — his proposed framing is to tell Musk: "we want to do OpenAI, if you want to do something inside Tesla, also fine. If weird competitive dynamics emerge, we'll re-discuss."
Ilya's meditating on "what we actually need." His concern is concrete: "don't want our only strategy to be dependent on Tesla. Don't want to be locked into a path where only Tesla works."
Then a Sam quote Greg captured — short, almost like a margin note:
"Sam: cares more about you two being motivated and not distracted. Usually a person can only fully focus on one thing. If you're not happy in non-profit, then we do B-corp. If you don't want E involved — my opinion is weak, doesn't matter compared to making both of you fully satisfied."
This passage almost never gets cited in any of the public narratives. But it might be the most important key to understanding Altman's strategy. He didn't say "I think Musk doesn't work." He didn't say "I want to be CEO." He said "your happiness matters most" — handing the decision to Ilya and Greg, while ensuring the decision they made was exactly the one he wanted.
Then comes the morning of Ilya and Greg getting breakfast together. Two guys — one chief scientist, one CTO — sitting in some SF coffee shop, arriving at the conclusion they'd been gestating for weeks:
"Conclusion is, we definitely want B-corp. Honestly, we also want to get back to actually working. But it's not clear how to get there."
Next entries — likely written late at night that November in Greg's SoMa apartment kitchen, or alone in the empty Pioneer Building office. Pacing slows. Heavier. Like a man wrestling with his own conscience:
"Can't say we promised non-profit. Don't want to say we promised. If three months from now we're doing B-corp, what we said before becomes a lie."
"His framing — that we never came clean about wanting a for-profit entity — will be correct."
Then the line that makes everyone pause:
"Stealing the non-profit from him is not okay. Converting it to B-corp without him — morally indefensible. And he's not stupid."
This entry matters because of what it proves. Greg and Ilya never lied to Musk. They didn't accept Musk's offer and then secretly pivot to B-corp. They rejected Musk's offer, precisely because they thought accepting and reversing later would be the actual betrayal.
Same entry has direct quotes from Musk that Greg captured — Musk's own words, not Greg's interpretation:
"Fundraising is too hard. I worked hard on Bill Gates, he wouldn't even come to the office. Dustin gave money but wouldn't come look. That's how much these people care. Didn't expect to be 70% of the cash. Thought I was just one of the donors. Website lists all these names — they didn't actually pay. Reality is, no one will give you that much money. When they see the number 10 billion, they get scared and run."
Then a Musk line Greg gave its own paragraph:
"Between the two of us, one of us isn't living in reality."
This sentence has two readings. Musk obviously meant: you think you can raise $10 billion? Dream on. But anyone reading this years later can't help reading it the other way too.
Greg also captured a surprising shift in Musk's tone — maybe the most unexpected detail of that whole period:
"But he was very supportive of us trying. Said, go for it, I'm happy. Anyone who wants to come — even just to be near me — I welcome them. Give them whatever senior title, lower my own title, I don't care. If you guys can pull this off, I want to learn from you. I'm good at getting other people to write checks — I can raise $1B for Tesla in a week, you guys see that company's financials?"
Greg's summary: "Either way, this is the best outcome possible from this meeting. We have direction now, we have a target. If we pull this off, we'll have surpassed him at the thing he's best at. If we fail — we'll cross that bridge."
Musk even gave them a concrete suggestion before leaving: do an ICO, target $1B raise. Said it'd involve a for-profit subsidiary.
But weeks later, Musk changed his mind again. Cooled on ICO (Greg and Ilya weren't bullish either). Pivoted to a more pessimistic take: "OpenAI is on a doomed path relative to Google."
This entry is the bloodiest battlefield between the two narratives.
Musk's courtroom narrative: his lawyers extracted three sentences — "can't say we promised non-profit" / "his framing will be correct" / "morally indefensible" — and stitched them into a picture of "insiders confessing betrayal." Filing headline: Brockman privately admits in his journal that they kept their plan secret from Musk.
OpenAI's counter: they posted the full journal. Pointed out the omitted context flips the meaning entirely. Greg and Ilya weren't plotting betrayal — they were debating whether to accept Musk's terms (extra board seats + lockup + non-compete), and ultimately didn't accept, because they thought accepting and later reversing would be the actual deception. "No one lied to Elon; no one ever told him they accepted the terms and then secretly reneged." The same journal entry has a priority list: "[1] B-corp with reasonable control structure [2] Non-profit with abundant funding [3] We resign and do it ourselves." And: "Answer is we prefer B-corp, but compared to Tesla, we prefer non-profit."
Karen Hao's third angle: Hao bypasses both legal frames. Her reading focuses on Altman's role in the journal. Sam's voice appears almost like a narrator — every appearance is "helping" Greg and Ilya think through their own thoughts, never directly stating his own position. Hao reads this as precision power-running: getting other people to make "their own choice" while ensuring that choice is yours. This technique is more advanced and harder to trace than open lobbying.
Act VI: the ultimatum
February 2018. SF, Pioneer Building.
Conference room is small — fits maybe 12. Seven people show up. Long white table. Projector screen on one wall (nobody's ever used it). Mission skyline through the window on the other.
By this point every possible path has been walked and looped back to start.
B-corp — collapsed because Musk demanded full control. Pure non-profit — Musk himself said it can't raise enough. Fold into Tesla — Greg and Ilya won't. January saw Musk briefly endorse an ICO to raise $1B, told the team "you've solved the long-term funding problem" — but pulled support by month-end and pivoted to "OpenAI is on a doomed path relative to Google."
Musk that month is darker. Model 3 hell ongoing. SpaceX Falcon Heavy successfully launched on Feb 6 (the famous flight with the Tesla Roadster). But his attention is fragmenting beyond useful allocation. In an email to Greg and Ilya he writes that he wants to hear details on the new fundraising plan they've been developing.
But in the same window, he's privately decided: OpenAI can't pull it off.
Musk arrives first. Wears a dark gray jacket — not his usual formal one, looks like something he grabbed from the closet. A detail later mentioned by someone in the room: Musk that day didn't look like a man arriving for a carefully prepared negotiation. He looked like a man arriving to execute a decision he'd already made.
Altman walks in with a Luckin iced americano (yes, Luckin). Greets everyone. Sits across from Musk. Puts the cup down.
Seating reconstructed by Hao: Musk at one end. Altman at the other. Greg in the middle, slightly Altman-side. Ilya in the middle, slightly Musk-side — but the physical "lean" no longer maps to any psychological allegiance.
First 20 minutes: routine items. Then Musk delivers his final read.
He tells the team he doesn't believe OpenAI can raise the necessary capital. He's leaving.
The phrasing on this final moment is where the two sides diverge subtly — but the subtlety hides completely different stories.
Hao's reconstruction (multi-source interviews): she calls it an "ultimatum." Musk says "either I run it, or I leave" — and every key person in the room has been pre-calibrated by Altman. Altman's response — "if that's your decision, we respect it" — is the cleanest cut in the entire chess game. It converts Musk's ultimatum from a weapon into a trap. Musk can't reverse without losing authority.
OpenAI's version: no "ultimatum." OpenAI's statement language: Musk quit, despite the team telling him they "had been developing a fundraising plan that didn't depend on a public offering, and would love his feedback." On the way out Musk said he "supported them pursuing the funding path they saw — just didn't believe they'd succeed, and would build AGI inside Tesla instead."
Note the wording difference: "ultimatum" implies expulsion. "Quit" implies voluntary departure. "Pre-calibrated" implies conspiracy. "We told him we had a plan" implies good faith.
Same event, same room, same evening. Did the man who walked out get pushed or did he choose? Depends on who you ask.
Per OpenAI's later account: Musk on the way out said he supported them trying their funding path. He just didn't think they'd make it. He'd put his energy into building AGI inside Tesla.
Musk stands up.
Doesn't slam the door. Doesn't raise his voice. Just stands there, gives Altman a long, unblinking stare.
Per multiple in-room sources to Hao, that stare contained a lot. Anger, definitely. Betrayal, obviously. But maybe something deeper, more disturbing:
Recognition.
Like the moment you realize what you thought was a window is actually a mirror, and the person in the mirror isn't you.
That 2015 blog post. The "deeply resonate" nods at Rosewood Sand Hill. The late-night text exchanges about AI doom. All those seemingly heart-to-heart resonances —
All mirrors. From day one Altman was reflecting Musk's own fear back at him, making him think they were on the same side.
Musk turns and walks out.
Nobody in the room speaks immediately. Greg takes off his glasses, cleans them slowly. Ilya stares at an equation still on the whiteboard like it's suddenly a language he doesn't recognize.
Altman picks up the iced americano, takes a sip. Lucky it was Luckin — anything else would've been watery by now.

Act VII: after the split — the war of two narratives
The first months after Musk left looked oddly calm.
Shivon Zilis — Musk's OpenAI liaison — joined the OpenAI board. This was a buffer in a sense: Musk was gone, but via Shivon he could still feel the room temperature. When Musk occasionally went off in private and made aggressive comments about OpenAI, Shivon helped the team "manage his emotions."
This delicate balance held for a while.
Then Musk started building xAI.
Shivon resigned from the OpenAI board. That was the signal: buffer removed. Musk no longer needed eyes inside OpenAI because he'd transitioned from observer to competitor.
What came next, traceable through Hao's investigation and OpenAI's own public statements, is a clear timeline: Musk launched a systematic, multi-front "harassment campaign" — public social media attacks, lawsuit after lawsuit, ongoing reputational damage.
OpenAI's January 2026 official response uses the word "harassment" directly. Says Musk's lawsuit is now in its fourth iteration, core purpose being to slow OpenAI down and create competitive runway for his xAI.
The most ironic part of the lawsuit: Musk in court claims he always insisted OpenAI must stay non-profit — but his own call recordings clearly show that in September 2017 he personally said to "transition from non-profit to B-corp or C-corp" and was "really excited" to push the change. He even personally created an OpenAI public benefit corp entity.
In court filings he selectively cites Greg Brockman's journal, extracts unfavorable-looking lines, systematically omits context. When the full journals dropped, they told a completely different story — not betrayal, but young people in an impossible bind trying to make morally correct choices.
But legal truth and public narrative are different things. Musk doesn't need to win the lawsuit. He needs to maintain a narrative: he's the betrayed idealist, OpenAI is the corrupted defector. That narrative gives xAI moral legitimacy and gives Musk's "anti-hero" public persona structural support.
Breaking down the narrative war:
Musk's arsenal: rounds of public social media attacks calling OpenAI a "closed-source profit-maximizing company entirely controlled by Microsoft." Lawsuit (4th amended version by Jan 2026) alleging breach of founding non-profit commitment. Selective citation of Greg's private journal, omitting context.
OpenAI's arsenal: January 2026 "The Truth Elon Left Out" public statement. Posts the full call transcripts and journal pages, color-marks Musk's lawyers' omissions. Frames his lawsuits as "harassment" and as "part of a broader strategy to gain competitive advantage for xAI." Points out Musk never actually treated OpenAI as an independent non-profit — from Autopilot to poaching Karpathy to trying to fold it into Tesla.
Karen Hao's third frame: Hao goes above the legal back-and-forth. Her core thesis isn't "who's right." It's: regardless of Musk vs Altman, both are deploying narratives that serve their own interests. Musk needs the "betrayed idealist" persona to legitimize xAI. Altman needs the "forced into commercialization" narrative to launder Microsoft's check. And in the collision of these two narratives, what actually got produced is something bigger than either of them.
Altman went to Microsoft.
First formal meeting at a low-key Microsoft SF office. Satya Nadella flew in personally. People in the room remember Nadella in his signature collarless sweater, that specific calm of the IIT-pedigree executive — not Musk's pre-launch tension, more like "whatever you say, I'll hear you out before deciding."
What Altman did to Nadella, structurally, was the same thing he did to Musk: identify the core need, become the perfect answer to it.
Nadella's need was clear: Microsoft was getting smoked by Google in AI. OpenAI was the answer.
First check: $1 billion. Eventually $13 billion.
After Musk's "between the two of us, one isn't in reality," Musk had given OpenAI 0% odds. Altman ended up making the OpenAI Foundation hold ~$130 billion of public benefit corp equity — on track to becoming the most resource-rich non-profit in history.
"Between the two of us, one of us isn't living in reality."
Years later, that line had a new answer.
Act VIII: the anxiety machine
November 30, 2022. SF, late night.
The moment ChatGPT went live wasn't technically dramatic. No rocket roar. No bell-ringing. A page just flipped from internal-test gray to public-access green. Engineer posted in Slack: "It's live." Someone responded with an emoji. Everyone went home.
Nobody saw what was coming.
Five days, 1M users. Two months, 100M users.
But the real game-changer wasn't user count. It was the specific chemical reaction this product triggered inside its users:
Anxiety.
A lawyer in Midtown drafting contracts realized ChatGPT could generate in 30 seconds what took him 2 hours. Closed the browser. Sat at his desk for 15 minutes, doing nothing.
A coder at Amazon HQ in Seattle threw a bug he was debugging at ChatGPT. Watched it return in 8 seconds the solution he'd missed all afternoon. Closed the laptop. Went to the bathroom and washed his face. The guy in the mirror looked like he'd just been told bad news.
A writing professor at a Boston university got a freshman essay so good he ran it through an AI detector. Detector said 98% AI-generated. Ran his own academic paper from the previous week through it. 74% AI-generated. He turned off the computer and sat in the dark.
This isn't normal product-launch excitement or curiosity. This is existential vertigo — "I thought only humans could do this and now machines can, possibly better than me."
The earthquake hit, with surgical precision, the highest-net-worth groups.
This is the commercial genius of the entire AI narrative.
Think back to 2016-2017 blockchain. What was the story? "Blockchain replaces banks." "Ethereum kills Visa." "Decentralization disrupts all of finance." That narrative built huge bubbles too — ICO mania, BTC at $20k. But it had a fundamental flaw:
The thing being "replaced" felt nothing.
Jamie Dimon never lost sleep over a crypto whitepaper from his JPM penthouse. Visa execs never seriously discussed Ethereum on a single earnings call. All those "ETH killers," "Visa killers," "Binance killers" — their anxiety yield was zero. When the side getting "killed" doesn't feel anxious, your story is an idling engine — noise, no torque.
AI is different.
AI's narrative isn't "replace some institution." It's "replace you." That "you" is humanity. But commercially, the smartest move is to target the highest-net-worth segment first — programmers making $200k+, lawyers billing in 6-minute increments, doctors at the top of the prestige hierarchy. When this group gets anxious, their anxiety doesn't quietly dissipate. They're information nodes: Twitter thought leaders, podcast regulars, the people media loves to interview. One SV engineer tweeting "my job might be replaced by AI" propagates 100x faster than a factory worker saying the same thing.
Anxiety transmission amplifies anxiety. Amplified anxiety drives procurement — companies don't want to fall behind, individuals don't want to be obsoleted. Procurement drives revenue. Revenue drives valuation. Valuation attracts capital. Capital trains stronger models. Stronger models manufacture more anxiety.
It's a perfect flywheel.
Its fuel isn't oil. Isn't data. Isn't compute.
Its fuel is human fear.
And critically — the people designing this flywheel know one thing: commercial selection isn't the most valuable direction. It's the highest fear-monetization-rate direction.
Have AI develop a cure for Alzheimer's? From a human welfare standpoint, maybe AI's most valuable application. But think about the anxiety yield: the beneficiaries are patients and families, their emotion is gratitude and hope, not fear. Gratitude doesn't drive arms races. Nobody's getting up at 3am to learn prompt engineering because "AI might cure Alzheimer's."
Have AI do basic physics, materials discovery, instrument design? From a developmental standpoint, 10,000x more meaningful than automating legal docs. But the audience is too narrow, net worth too distributed, anxiety propagation chain too long.
So AI's first "attack" was coding, law, finance, medical imaging, copywriting — every prestige white-collar job. Not because these fields needed AI most. Because when these workers get anxious, their fear is the most monetizable.
You feel pain → you start chasing like a maniac — buying courses, tools, subscriptions, anything claiming to keep you "from being left behind." And the people in front, watching the wave of crazed chasers behind them, also start running like maniacs — releasing stronger models, announcing bigger raises, claiming more dramatic breakthroughs.
Tech has no inherent direction. Commerce does.
Act IX: the rhythm of pain
But manufacturing anxiety is a precision craft. Too much, you crash. Too little, you're cold.
The subtlety: vendors need to maintain a precise rhythm — pain has to release continuously, but never all at once (then the story's over) and not too rarely (then you're the next blockchain).
Look at AI release cadence: GPT-3 surprised people, GPT-3.5 made them anxious, GPT-4 made them fear. Each iteration pushes one step forward — never to the finish line. Each release is an anxiety booster shot — strong enough that you think "holy shit, this time it's different," but leaves enough imperfection that you know "the next gen will be even stronger."
This is why the definition of AGI is deliberately kept vague.
Karen Hao flags this specifically in her book: because AGI has no scientific definition, the goalposts can move infinitely. If you define the endpoint, two outcomes: either you reach it — anxiety releases, narrative ends, valuation tops; or you never reach it — anxiety gets falsified, narrative collapses, bubble pops. But if you never define the endpoint? You stay in "almost there" forever. Each new model release: "we're another step closer to AGI."
The finish line stays on the horizon. You sprint forever. Anxiety never expires.
Sam Altman is a master of this. Every line from his public interviews is precision-calibrated. "We've seen surprising emergent capabilities" — vague enough to maintain mystery, specific enough to manufacture fear. "Some of GPT-4's behavior surprised even us" — simultaneously demonstrates technical uncontrollability (raises fear), implies team humility (lowers hostility), maintains AI's mystical aura (protects valuation).
Joseph Weizenbaum at MIT — inventor of ELIZA, the first chatbot in the 1960s — became one of AI's sharpest critics late in life. He said something Hao puts on the book's epigraph:
AI is amazing — but the moment you explain how it works, the magic shatters. It's just "a collection of programs."
OpenAI's business model in some sense is making sure that magic never shatters. When people think AI is a digital brain on the verge of awakening, the "replace you" narrative has power. If everyone knew it was just a statistical engine doing probability-max next-token prediction — like Ilya's whiteboard meditation that never has a confirmed answer — fear gets cut in half. So does valuation.
Mystery is the preservative for anxiety.
Act X: the empire's basement
Summer 2024. Kenya. Outside Nairobi.
In an unair-conditioned office, ~30 young people sit at desks arranged like an exam room. Each has an old laptop in front of them. Their job: data labeling. Using human judgment to teach AI which responses are appropriate, which content is harmful.
These people make less than $2/hour.
Many of them spend full days viewing hundreds of images and texts containing violence, porn, hate speech, and labeling them. One labeler told an investigative reporter he has nightmares — the images from his day chasing him into sleep. But he can't quit. In Nairobi's job market, this is one of the better jobs available.
Hao cites a story from New York Magazine: a data-labeling mom, terrified of missing the brief low-pay tasks on the platform, screams at her kid when he runs over. Cries afterward. Says she feels like a "monster" — her humanity being slowly squeezed out of her, fed to a machine she'll never use and never understand.
On the other side of the planet — Chile, near the Atacama desert — AI companies are trying to extract massive amounts of fresh water to cool datacenter servers. The communities there already face severe water shortage. Residents found out from local newspapers that someone was coming for their water.
In Memphis, Tennessee — a Southern city famous for blues, BBQ, and a deep history of racial segregation — Musk's xAI built a supercomputer center called "Colossus" in 2024. Sited in a working-class, mostly Black and brown community.
 
How did the community find out? They smelled gas drifting through their living rooms.
Colossus uses 35 methane gas turbines for power. The exhaust pumps directly into a community already struggling with industrial pollution. Respiratory disease, asthma, lung cancer — all already higher than national averages. Now add 35 more turbines.
When Memphis residents gathered at city hall to protest, when Chilean farmers challenged water-rights approvals in court, when Nairobi labelers quietly told their stories to journalists — none of them knew about each other. None of them knew they were on different floors of the same imperial structure.
Hao calls this "AI colonialism" and breaks it into four pillars — resource extraction, labor exploitation, physical extraction, hierarchy logic — that map exactly onto historical colonial empires.
Anxiety sells up. Pain shifts down.
The upper architecture is SV magic narrative + trillion-dollar valuations. The lower foundation is the invisible global supply chain — the people whose net worth is too low, whose anxiety doesn't pay, whose pain is a rounding error in the commercial model.
Worth flagging: on this dimension, Hao's narrative and OpenAI's legal filings don't have "two versions." OpenAI's official statement focuses entirely on its legal fight with Musk — who said what, who betrayed whom, who has authority over what. Its narrative boundary is the SV boardroom and the Delaware court. Hao's narrative penetrates several continents below that. The Nairobi labelers, the Chilean water, the Memphis air — none of these people, none of these places appear in OpenAI's statement. Not even once. This isn't an oversight. It's a structural difference in field of vision. When you're litigating a trillion-dollar empire, the colonies are out of frame by definition. What Hao did was turn the camera toward what was excluded.
This is the empire's full structure.
Epilogue
That February 2018 night, when Musk walked out of Pioneer Building, the SF sky was one of the unremarkable ones — turned gray-yellow by office park and tower lights, not a single star visible.
He pulled out his phone. Mission Street had occasional cars passing, tire-on-wet-pavement sound like a low sigh.
He thought he was losing control of a company. He didn't know he was losing definitional power over an entire narrative.
From that moment, the AI story stopped being "how do we make AI safe" — that was Musk's story, with stance, lines, and blind spots. It became "how AI will reshape everything" — that was Altman's story. No endpoint, no lines, just precisely calibrated rhythm and a horizon that retreats forever.
But the facts — Greg's late-night journal entries, the September 2017 three-way call recording, every email surfaced through legal discovery — are messier than either side's narrative.
Musk isn't a pure victim. He pushed for-profit conversion himself. Demanded full control. Wanted his kids to inherit AGI. Wanted OpenAI as a Mars-program puzzle piece. Treated it as Tesla's tech outsource shop while it was still a non-profit. In court he sliced Greg's journal to construct the "betrayed" narrative, omitting every line where he himself had championed the conversion.
Altman isn't a pure conspirator. Greg's journals show painful moral calculation at every step. They rejected Musk's terms precisely because they didn't want to fake-accept and quietly reverse. Ilya literally felt morally obligated to make non-profit work.
But what Hao saw — above the legal fight — is another layer of reality: regardless of who's right or wrong, what did this war between two men actually produce?
A global anxiety flywheel.
Musk launching xAI, building Colossus, polluting Memphis air with 35 gas turbines — he thinks he's counterattacking Altman. Altman taking Microsoft's $13B, dropping stronger models in waves — he thinks he's executing the mission. But the methods they're using — bigger compute, faster models, harder deployment — are structurally identical.
A man who believes in fear and a man who sells fear, both ended up feeding the same machine.
And right now that machine is running at full speed across the planet.
In SF towers, it exists as trillion-dollar valuations. In NY law firms, it exists as a partner staring blankly at ChatGPT-generated contracts for five minutes. In Nairobi labeling offices, it exists as a young man going home and throwing up after a full day of extreme violence imagery. In Memphis communities, it exists as an old person closing the windows but still smelling gas. In drought-hit Chilean villages, it exists as a well dropping another three inches.
It doesn't care if you're a coder or a labeler. Doesn't care if you're in SV or Nairobi. Doesn't care if your anxiety is about unemployment or breathing.
It calculates one thing only:
Can your fear be turned into money?
If yes — welcome aboard. You're a user, a customer, a positive number in the valuation model.
If no — you're the fuel. Your data, your labor, your water, your air, all quietly extracted, all converted into invisible kinetic energy for the flywheel.
And the man who in 2015 wrote "AI is the biggest threat to humanity," and the man who in 2018 walked out in anger — neither of them stopped this flywheel.
Both became part of it.
